# The five friend chats

**One page. Have it open on your phone.** Ten minutes each, over a coffee you were having anyway. Five people is enough — past that you stop hearing new things.

---

## Picking the five

Two rules, and they take about two minutes against your contacts list.

1. **They live in Manhattan and would plausibly join.** Someone who'd never use it gives you polite answers.
2. **Make them different from each other.** Two settled and comfortable, two younger and renting, one you're not sure about. That spread is the whole point — you're trying to find out who this is really for, and five versions of the same person won't tell you.

Don't pick five people who'll be nice to you. Pick at least one who'll tell you it's a bad idea.

---

## The five questions

### 1. "When did you last get burned, or nearly burned, buying or selling or hiring someone in the city?"

Opens with their story, not your idea. You want a specific incident with a number attached, not "oh Craigslist is terrible."

**Listening for:** which of the three things it was — buying something, renting somewhere, hiring a person. That tells you where the real pain is, which may not be where you've pointed the product.

### 2. "If there were a members-only Manhattan site, what's the first thing you'd search for?"

**Listening for:** the first noun out of their mouth. If four of five say a person — a cleaner, a mover, a piano teacher — that's a louder signal than any opinion they give you later, because it's what they'd actually do rather than what they think of your plan.

### 3. "Which of these three grabs you: discounts at local places, vetted people you can hire, or buying and selling with neighbours who've been vouched for?"

**Listening for:** the one they react to before they've thought about it. Watch their face, not their answer. Ask which one they'd use *this month*.

### 4. "What would make an invitation to something like this feel special rather than spammy?"

**Listening for:** their exact words. Write them down verbatim — this is the raw material for the invitation itself, and people are much better at describing what would feel right than at telling you whether your idea is good.

### 5. "Who are the three Manhattanites you'd vouch for?"

The standing closer. Ask it every time, even if the rest of the chat went badly.

**Listening for:** three names. Any hesitation about putting their name to someone is itself the product working — that hesitation is exactly what keeps the network good.

---

## While you're there: the tagline test

Say these out loud, in the middle of the conversation, and watch their face. Don't ask which they prefer — people are terrible at that and will pick the longest one.

- "No scams, no spam, no strangers."
- "Every member vouched for."
- "New York's trusted private marketplace."

The one that makes them nod, or repeat it back, wins.

---

## Writing it down

Five minutes after each one, before you forget. Straight into a note or send it to me and I'll file it.

- Their name, and which bucket they fell into (settled / younger / unsure)
- The burn story, in one line
- The first thing they said they'd search for
- Which of the three grabbed them
- Their words on what makes an invite feel right
- The three names

---

## What this actually decides

Two things have been waiting on these conversations since 13 August:

**Who Manhattanite is for.** Older and settled, or younger and renting. Every messaging decision sits on top of this one and it's currently open.

**Whether the three-pillar framing is real.** Discounts, services, buying and selling. It's a warmer pitch than "private classifieds," but only one of the three is actually built. Question 3 tells you whether people want the other two enough to justify building them.

Five coffees answers both.
